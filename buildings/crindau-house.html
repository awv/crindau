Crindau House

Late sixteenth-century regional stone manor house, agrarian seat of the Herbert lineage and precursor to industrial Crindau.

Origins & Manorial Heritage

Crindau House stands as a remarkable, solitary survivor of the rural agrarian landscape that existed long before the brick terraces of Albany Street and the chimneys of the South Wales Glass Works hemmed it in. Erected around 1580 during the reign of Elizabeth I, it was constructed as the substantial regional stone seat of the prominent Herbert family.

The building conforms to the classic regional plan of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, featuring massive rubble stone walling, square-headed windows with moulded stone dressings, and high, steep gables. At the time of its construction, the estate commanded wide, pastoral marshes sloping down toward the tidal bends of the River Usk.

"An ancient domestic seat of considerable regional importance, demonstrating the wealth of Monmouthshire gentry prior to modern urban expansion."

The Industrial Encroachment

During the nineteenth century, the tranquil rural character of Crindau dissolved. The construction of the Newport gasworks, railway spurs, and the Monmouthshire Canal encircled the historic grounds. The open meadows were parcelled off into building leases, creating the street grids now recorded in the commercial directories.

Despite severe surrounding pressures and decades of heavy rail traffic yards from its doorstep, the primary domestic block survived largely intact, receiving Grade II* listed protection in recognition of its surviving post-medieval fabric.
